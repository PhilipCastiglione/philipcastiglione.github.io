Garden Pods in Ireland — Research

Researched 2026-09-18, revised 2026-09-19.

Brief: a ~12m² (3m × 4m) garden pod in Raheny, Dublin 5, usable as either a gym or an office, with the use likely to switch between the two over the years. 3-bed semi-detached / end-of-terrace, built 2006. 43.075m² of rear open space. The pod goes to the rear, on the footprint of an existing 1.8m × 2.35m shed that is being removed. No plumbing. Wired Ethernet wanted.

Constraints that shape everything below:

  • Turnkey only. No managing contractors, no self-finishing, no coordinating a base with one firm and a build with another. One supplier, one contract, one number.
  • Republic of Ireland suppliers only. No cross-border currency, VAT or consumer-rights complications.
  • No maintenance-hungry materials. Nothing that needs oiling or re-treating on a cycle.
  • Quality and reliability beat price. A 10–15% price difference is noise across a structure that will sit in the garden for decades. Degradation, defects, and a supplier who disappears are not.

Options that fail these filters are in the appendices, kept for reference and price context.

A turnkey 12m² pod from a Republic of Ireland supplier lands at €18,000–€22,000 for a well-specified one, rising to €25k+ for premium. Published prices at almost exactly your size:

✓ Key finding

Regulatory position: clear. At 12m² you’re inside every threshold with room to spare, and there’s nothing to file, notify or certify apart from the electrical work. Full detail in §7 if you want it; you don’t need to act on any of it.

The real decision is which supplier, not which pod. The products in the €18–20k turnkey band are more alike than different. What varies is whether the company turns up, finishes properly, and is still answering the phone in three years. §2 is about how to judge that.

2. How to actually choose a supplier

The hard part is that every supplier’s website looks the same: nice photos, happy testimonials, no prices, no specifics. Here’s a way to get past that without spending weeks on it.

2.1 Hard filters — apply these first, they eliminate most of the market

Bin a supplier immediately if any of these are true. No further thought required.

  • Not based in the Republic of Ireland.
  • Not genuinely turnkey — if the base, the fit-out or the electrical connection is “your side”, it’s out.
  • Won’t take on removing the existing shed as part of their scope (§6.1 explains why this matters more than it sounds).
  • Cladding requires periodic oiling or re-treating and they can’t offer a maintenance-free alternative.
  • Asks for full payment up front, or offers a notable discount conditional on paying early or in full.
  • Won’t put the quote, the scope and the warranty in writing.

2.2 The quote process is the test

✓ Key finding — the quote process is the test

This is the single most useful idea in this document. How a supplier behaves while trying to win your business is the best available predictor of how they’ll behave once they have your deposit — and it costs you nothing but a few emails to find out.

The detailed one-star review of The Pod Factory (§4) describes a 12-week promise becoming seven months, with replies averaging over two weeks. That behaviour was almost certainly visible during the sales process. Watch for:

  • Response time to your first email. If it takes a week now, it’ll take three when there’s a problem.
  • Do they visit the site, or quote blind? A site visit means they’ve actually looked at access, ground conditions, and where the cable trench will run. A blind quote means variations later.
  • Is the quote itemised, or one number with no breakdown? Itemised means they’ve costed the job. A bare number means scope arguments when the shed slab turns out to be thicker than expected.
  • Do they answer a direct question directly? “What’s the insulation thickness?” should get a number, not “it’s fully insulated to the highest standard”.
  • Do they volunteer problems? A supplier who says “that base might need extra depth given where the old shed was” is thinking about your job. One who says everything is fine, always, isn’t.
  • Do they push for a quick decision or deposit? Urgency manufactured by a supplier is a bad sign, and you have no deadline.

Two or three of these going badly is enough to walk away, even from the cheapest quote.

2.4 Where price actually comes in

Use price twice, and only twice:

  1. To spot an outlier. Something 40% below the field isn’t a bargain, it’s a different specification — thinner insulation, no base, no electrical connection, or a shell rather than a finished room. Find out which before dismissing or accepting it.
  2. To break a tie between two suppliers you’re equally happy with.

That’s it. Do not rank on price.

2.5 Showrooms — your best substitute for seeing a real install

You can’t ask a supplier for the addresses of previous customers; handing those out would breach data-protection rules, and any supplier who agreed would be doing the same with your address later.

The legitimate equivalent is a showroom visit, and several suppliers have them within easy reach:

  • Shanette — Dublin 12
  • My Garden Room — showrooms in Dublin, Kildare and Wicklow
  • Loghouse — Rathcoole, Co. Dublin
  • Steeltech — Dublin among many locations

You can do two or three in an afternoon. Twenty minutes inside an actual unit tells you more about finish quality, ceiling height, door and window feel, and how solid the thing is than any amount of web research. Push on the walls. Look at the corners and the junction where the roof meets the wall. Ask them to show you the same model you’d be buying, not the top-of-range display piece.

2.6 Suggested approach, given you're time-poor

  1. Visit two showrooms in one afternoon (Shanette in Dublin 12 and My Garden Room are the easiest pairing).
  2. Request quotes from four suppliers with the same brief (§8). Sprout Pod, Loghouse, Modern Garden Rooms and Garden Office Solutions is a reasonable set — see §3 for why.
  3. Score them on §2.2 behaviour as the quotes come in. Expect one or two to self-eliminate by being slow or evasive.
  4. Run the Companies Registration Office check (§4.3) on your final two.
  5. Choose on quality, track record and how they’ve dealt with you. Use price to break the tie.

4.3 Company health — the Companies Registration Office check

Worth doing on your final two, and it takes about ten minutes.

How it works: the register is at core.cro.ie. Searching by company name or number is free and needs no account. The documents themselves — annual returns and the financial statements attached to them — are paid, per document.

What to look at, in order:

  1. Incorporation date. How long have they actually traded? This is free to check and is the single most useful number. A company trading since 2008 has survived a property crash and a pandemic.
  2. Are annual returns filed, and filed on time? Also free to see. Persistent late filing is a reasonable proxy for a business under strain or badly run.
  3. The most recent financial statements, if you want to pay for one document. Look at net assets and whether directors have loaned money in.
  4. Trading name vs registered entity. Most of these operate under a brand that isn’t the registered company name. The name on your contract and the name on the quote should match a real registered entity — if you can’t find one, ask them directly which company you’d be contracting with.

Important limitation: filed accounts can already be over a year out of date by the time you read them. Companies get 56 days after their annual return date to file, and the statements themselves can be nine months old at that point. Treat this as one input, not an answer.

4.4 Deposits — what's normal, what's a red flag, and what protection you have

You’re not in a position to dictate payment terms to a supplier, and you shouldn’t try. A deposit is normal and reasonable in this trade. The realistic approach is to know what a bad request looks like and simply not use that supplier.

⚠ Warning — deposit risk

Why it matters: the pattern to avoid is DK Windows & Doors — a Dublin 12 firm trading since 2005, into liquidation in December 2024 having accumulated €1.5 million in customer deposits, days after a Black Friday push encouraging large deposits. Customers became unsecured creditors, at the bottom of the queue. One couple faced losing close to €50,000. Nothing garden-room-specific has come to light, but the shape of the risk is identical.

⚠ Warning — red flags

Red flags — walk away:

  • Full payment demanded up front.
  • A meaningful discount conditional on paying early or in full.
  • Pressure to pay a deposit before you have a written quote and scope.
  • No written contract.
  • Cash or bank transfer pushed specifically as a way to get a better price.

☐ Action — protection you actually have

The protection you actually have, and it’s better than I first said: pay by card. The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission confirms that chargeback applies to Irish debit cards, not just credit cards — if you pay a deposit by debit or credit card and the business doesn’t deliver, you can ask your bank to reverse the transaction. A bank transfer has no equivalent remedy. So:

  • Pay the deposit by debit card rather than bank transfer, even if it’s slightly less convenient.
  • Keep the written quote and scope, since a chargeback claim needs evidence of what was promised.
  • Don’t pay the final tranche until the Safe Electric Completion Certificate is in your hands and the snag list is closed.

If a supplier won’t take card for a deposit, that’s information too.

5. Designing it for both gym and office

Since the use will switch, the right approach is to spec each aspect for whichever use is more demanding. That costs very little extra and avoids a room that only works one way.

Aspect More demanding use Spec for it
Insulation & heating Office Sitting still for eight hours in January needs far more heat than a workout does. Office use sets the insulation requirement.
Ventilation Gym Exercise generates heat and moisture fast; a sealed, well-insulated box gets humid and then condenses. Gym use sets the ventilation requirement.
Ceiling height Gym 2.4m internal for overhead lifts — and it makes a much better office than a low one anyway.
Floor Gym Must take dropped weights without flexing or drumming.
Glazing Both, differently Office needs glare control; gym wants usable wall space. See below.

5.1 Getting both right

Ceiling height: 2.4m internal, and confirm it in writing. Ask for internal floor-to-ceiling height, not wall height and not external height — those are different numbers and suppliers quote whichever flatters them. The budget steel units are 2.1m, which is genuinely tight for anything overhead and impossible to fix later. This is the one dimension with no remedy.

Floor: concrete base, then rubber matting. A concrete slab handles dropped weights without flex or noise; a timber deck on joists over ground screws is springier and drummier. Rubber matting over the slab can be lifted or covered with a rug when it’s an office. Don’t let anyone fit built-in furniture — it locks in one use.

Ventilation: an openable window plus a small extract fan. This is the most commonly skipped item and the most common regret. It’s also cheap. The gym case demands it; the office case benefits from it on a warm afternoon.

Glazing: moderate, and think about which way it faces. A full glass frontage looks great in photos and causes two real problems — glare on monitors, and summer overheating in a small insulated box. It also removes the wall space a gym wants for a rack or mirrors. Ask where the glazing will face relative to the sun, and plan a desk position that isn’t looking into it or backing onto it.

Heating: one panel heater is plenty at 12m² if the insulation is decent. If the insulation is thin, you pay for that every winter instead. This is the insulation decision showing up as a running cost.

Acoustics — the office case, and it’s not what you’d think. You don’t need sound isolation from the neighbours; normal construction is fine and that’s what everyone does. What you may notice is the inside of a small hard-surfaced box echoing on video calls. A rug and something soft on one wall fixes it entirely. Worth knowing, not worth engineering.

Power: more sockets than you think, at two heights. Desk-height sockets for an office, floor-level for gym equipment and a fan. Sockets are cheap now and expensive once the walls are finished. Ethernet is covered in §7.4.

6. What differs between the offerings

6.1 Removing the shed is on the critical path

The shed (1.8m × 2.35m, about 4.2m²) sits exactly where the pod goes, so its removal isn’t tidying up — it gates the whole job.

  • Keep it inside one contract. If the pod company owns demolition, disposal, base and install as a single scope, there’s no gap for anyone to blame anyone else, and you’re not coordinating it.
  • Ask what’s under it. A shed of that age very likely sits on a slab or paving. Get breaking it out and removing the spoil explicitly priced, and settle who bears the cost if it’s thicker than expected — that’s the classic mid-job variation. Don’t let anyone build on the old base; a shed slab won’t have the depth, reinforcement or level tolerance a pod needs.
  • Trace the existing electrical supply before anyone digs. The shed has power of unknown provenance. The electrician should find and isolate it at the board before demolition. Its route may also be the cheapest path for the new cable.

6.2 Cladding and maintenance — a correction worth reading

You said you’d never oil wood, and that’s a reasonable filter. But the picture is more forgiving than “wood = ongoing work”:

✓ Key finding — cedar silvering

Cedar and larch left alone weather to a silver-grey within a few years, and this does not affect durability at all. Cedar has a 25+ year maintenance-free life. The oiling every 18 months is purely cosmetic — it preserves the original honey colour. If you’re happy with silver-grey, cedar is effectively maintenance-free.

  • Fibre cement (brands: Cedral, James Hardie) is genuinely zero-maintenance — immune to rot, fungus and insects, no treatment ever, and currently cheaper than cedar. This is the strongest match for your requirement. My Garden Room uses it.
  • Composite holds its chosen colour for years without treatment and doesn’t silver. Sprout Pod offers it as an option.
  • PVC-coated steel (Shanette, and the budget units) needs no painting or treatment.

So the real question to ask a cedar-clad supplier is: “if I never treat this, what does it look like in five years, and does anything fail?” The honest answer is “grey, and no”. Decide whether you’re fine with grey. If not, pick fibre cement or composite and the question disappears.

6.3 Insulation — the number to ask for

Insulation depth is the biggest genuine difference between a room you use in January and one you avoid. Ask for thickness in millimetres for walls, roof and floor. It’s a number anyone in the trade can give you on the spot, and it’s directly comparable across suppliers.

Rough scale:

  • 40mm composite panel — the budget tier. Fine for storage, thin for all-day winter occupancy.
  • 80–100mm — Shanette’s upper options. A reasonable working standard.
  • 150mm — Modern Garden Rooms’ wall build-up. Approaching a proper house wall.
  • Structural Insulated Panels — Garden Office Solutions. Perform well for their thickness and are airtight, which makes the ventilation point in §5.1 more important, not less.

(You may see “U-values” mentioned in the trade — a measure of heat loss where lower is better. Almost nobody in this sector publishes them and asking for them on every component will make you look like hard work. Millimetres of insulation is the practical question.)

6.5 Warranties and lead times

Warranties are inconsistent and mostly unpublished. The outliers are Concept Modular (50-year structural) and Garden Room Ireland (25-year exterior). Get it in writing with the quote and check what it actually covers — structure only, or also the roof membrane, glazing units and electrics, which are what actually fail. Weigh the length against how long the company has existed.

Lead times of 10–12 weeks are typical, and Garden Office Solutions runs a 3–4 month queue. You have no deadline, so this is not a constraint — but be suspicious of anyone promising dramatically faster than the field. The Pod Factory complaint was exactly that: 12 weeks promised, seven months delivered.

6.6 Design service

You don’t need one and shouldn’t pay for one. For a 12m² exempt structure there is nothing to design around — no drawings to lodge, no certificates to file. Most suppliers do a free site visit and 3D visuals as part of quoting.

7. Regulations — settled, for reference

Nothing here needs action from you beyond the electrical work in §7.4. Included so the basis is on record.

7.1 Planning — exempt

Planning and Development Regulations 2001, Schedule 2, Part 1, Class 3, as substituted by S.I. 338/2026, in force 27 July 2026. Verbatim conditions:

Class 3 conditions (Schedule 2, Part 1, as substituted by S.I. 338/2026)

  1. No such structure forward of the front wall of a house.
  2. Total area of such structures, taken together with any other such structures previously constructed, shall not exceed 30 square metres.
  3. Shall not reduce private open space reserved exclusively for the occupants, to the rear or to the side, to less than 25 square metres.
  4. External finishes of any structure placed to the side of a house (and tiled or slated roof covering) shall conform with those of the house.
  5. Height not to exceed 4m for a tiled or slated pitched roof, or 3m in any other case.
  6. Not to be used for human habitation, nor for keeping pigs, poultry, pigeons, ponies or horses, nor for any purpose other than one incidental to the enjoyment of the house.

✓ Key finding — exemption headroom

Applied to you: 12m² against a 30m² cap, with the shed gone — about 18m² spare. Retained open space 43.075 − 12 = 31.08m² against a 25m² minimum. Condition 4 doesn’t apply because the pod is to the rear, so cladding is a free choice. Comfortable on every count.

⚠ Warning — 3m height cap

The only one to actually watch: condition 5. A flat or membrane roof caps you at 3m, not 4m — the 4m allowance applies only to a tiled or slated pitched roof. Confirm the quoted overall height measured from finished ground level including the base. Most pods are 2.5–3m, so this is a check, not a problem.

The 2026 instrument also raised the house-extension exemption from 40m² to 45m², and separate instruments created a new Class 3A for habitable garden dwellings (32–45m², 14 days’ notice to the council, expires 31 December 2030). Not your use case, but note a Class 3 structure eats into the combined allowance if you ever wanted a granny flat.

7.2 Building Regulations — exempt

Building Regulations 1997, Third Schedule (Exempted Buildings), Class 2 exempts a single-storey detached building ancillary to a dwelling, not exceeding 25m², not exceeding 3m (4m pitched), used for recreational or storage purposes and not for a trade or business.

A 12m² personal gym or a home office for your own use sits comfortably inside this. The trade-or-business condition is aimed at running a business from the building — clients visiting, staff, signage — not at working from home. Garden offices are ubiquitous in Dublin and this is not a live issue.

One quirk worth knowing if you ever scale up: planning exemption is now 30m² but the Building Regulations exemption is still 25m², so a pod between the two would be planning-exempt and Building-Regs-caught. At 12m² you’re inside both.

7.3 Commencement notice — not required

Building Control Regulations 1997, Article 7: commencement notices apply only to buildings to which the Building Regulations apply, and are excluded where the works are exempted development and fire safety certification doesn’t apply. They’re re-applied only to a new dwelling or an extension over 45m².

✓ Key finding — no commencement notice required

A detached 12m² garden pod is neither. No commencement notice, no filing, no assigned certifier, no fee.

Some supplier blogs claim otherwise — they’ve imported the rule for the new Class 3A habitable units.

7.4 Electrical — the one part with real obligations

  • This is “restricted electrical work” under the Electricity Regulation Act 1999. It must be done by a Registered Electrical Contractor (REC) on the Safe Electric scheme, regulated by the Commission for Regulation of Utilities. Unregistered work is a prosecutable offence.
  • The contractor must issue you a Completion Certificate. This is your proof the installation meets the National Rules for Electrical Installations. Get it before final payment.
  • Correct method: a dedicated circuit from the house consumer unit — not a spur off an existing socket — in steel wire armoured (SWA) cable, buried 450–600mm on a sand bed with warning tape above, terminating in a consumer unit inside the pod with residual-current protection.
  • Your 2006 board probably has a spare way and spare capacity, but it should be confirmed rather than discovered on install day.
  • The existing shed supply is of unknown provenance — treat it as suspect. Have it tested; don’t let anyone reuse it to save a trench unless it tests clean and is certified.

Ethernet: run it in the same trench in separate ducting, physically separated from the power cable. Pull a spare draw-cord or empty duct at the same time — it costs almost nothing while the trench is open and buys every future option. Retrofitting means digging the garden up again.

7.5 Loose ends

  • Drainage. Your purchase pack should have a certified site layout showing foul and surface-water runs. Worth a glance before fixing the pod position and trench route.
  • Roof runoff. 12m² is a small volume but needs a destination — a soakaway or the existing surface-water system, not toward a boundary.
  • Home insurance. Notify your insurer. Outbuildings are often covered only to a sub-limit and gym equipment may need specifying.

8. What to talk to suppliers about

Your list, with the reasoning and a few additions. Send roughly this as the initial enquiry — it’s specific without being an interrogation, and the way it’s answered is itself the test (§2.2).

The brief to give them:

Appendix A — Budget and self-finish options (ruled out)

Kept for price context. All fail the turnkey requirement, and the 2.1m wall heights rule them out for gym use regardless.

Supplier Price at ~12m² Why it’s out
Adman Steel Sheds €11,250 (3m × 4.2m) 40mm insulation, 2.1m wall height, no base, a bare oriented strand board (OSB) floor with no covering, “ready to be connected to mains” — you arrange the connection. 10–12 week lead time. Full price list published.
Steeltech From €10,490 40mm steel composite panel, factory-wired plug-and-play. boards.ie comment: “looked and felt cheap”. Showrooms nationwide including Dublin.
Urban Garden Sheds €3,350–€11,395 range 25mm or 40mm insulation, PVC-coated steel (no painting), 10-year guarantee on frame and cladding, 84 five-star Google reviews. Shed-grade rather than room-grade; electrical work is an optional extra. Leinster delivery.
Gardenhouse24 / Summerhouse24 ~€5,600–6,000 for a 12m² cabin Baltic log cabin kits, not insulated year-round rooms. Not comparable. See the aftercare complaints in §4.1.

The one worth a second look: Shanette started as a steel shed manufacturer but its 80mm and 100mm specifications include slabbing, plastering, painting and flooring — which makes it genuinely turnkey. It’s in the main list in §3 for that reason. Just don’t let anyone quote you the 40mm version.

The useful number from this tier: My Garden Room publishes both a shell price (€9,550) and a turnkey price (€19,600) for the same 4.5m × 3m unit. That €10,000 gap is what turnkey actually costs, and it’s why shell prices aren’t comparable to finished ones.
