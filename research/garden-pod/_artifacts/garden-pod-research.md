# Garden Pods in Ireland — Research

Researched 2026-09-18. Context: ~12m² (3m × 4m) garden gym, backup use as office. Raheny, Dublin 5. 3-bed semi-d / end-of-terrace, built 2006. 43.075m² rear open space (52.955m² including the side lane, which is long and narrow and not a candidate location). Pod to be sited to the rear, where the existing ~3m² shed stands; that shed is being removed. No plumbing wanted; Ethernet desirable.

---

## 1. Bottom line

For a 12m² insulated garden gym in Dublin, the realistic market is:

| Tier | All-in cost | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| Budget steel shell | €11k–14k | 40mm composite panel, 2.1m wall height, factory-wired but not connected, no base, no floor covering |
| Turnkey mid-market | €18k–22k | Properly insulated build-up, finished interior, sockets/lights/heating/flooring, often base included |
| Premium / bespoke | €25k–35k+ | SIPs or deep timber build-up, cedar cladding, triple glazing, architect-ish detailing |

Plus, where not included: concrete base (€1.5k–2.5k for 12m²), electrical supply from the house (~€900–1,500), Ethernet (~€400), skip/shed removal (~€350).

**Your regulatory position is comfortable.** At 12m² you are inside every relevant threshold by a wide margin — planning exemption, Building Regulations exemption, and commencement-notice exemption all apply. Details in §2.

**Two things you probably don't know yet:** the exemption limit changed on 27 July 2026 (25m² → 30m²), and the *Building Regulations* exemption hinges on the building being used for *recreational* purposes and **not** "for the purposes of any trade or business" — which is exactly the gym-vs-office distinction you raised. See §2.2.

## 2. Regulations

### 2.1 Planning — you are exempt

Governing law: Planning and Development Regulations 2001 (S.I. 600/2001), Schedule 2, Part 1, **Class 3**, as substituted by **S.I. 338/2026**, in force **27 July 2026**.

The current Class 3 conditions, verbatim from the statutory instrument:

1. No such structure forward of the front wall of the house.
2. Total area of such structures, **taken together with any other such structures previously constructed**, shall not exceed **30 square metres**.
3. Shall not reduce private open space reserved exclusively for the occupants, to the rear or to the side, to less than **25 square metres**.
4. External finishes of any structure **placed to the side of a house** (and tiled/slated roof covering) shall conform with those of the house.
5. Height not to exceed **4m** for a tiled or slated pitched roof, or **3m in any other case**.
6. Not to be used for human habitation, nor for keeping pigs/poultry/pigeons/ponies/horses, nor "for any other purpose other than a purpose incidental to the enjoyment of the house as such."

**Applied to you:**

- Cumulative area: the shed is being removed (it occupies the pod's footprint), so the cumulative figure is **12m² against a 30m² cap** — about 18m² spare. You could go considerably larger if you ever wanted to, and you retain headroom for a future shed or store.
- Retained open space: 43.075 − 12 = **31.08m²** against a 25m² minimum. Comfortable, and this is the conservative figure — condition 3 counts private open space "to the rear **or to the side**", so the lane arguably adds to the total. You don't need it to comply.
- Height: most pods are 2.5–3m. A flat or EPDM roof caps you at **3m**, not 4m — the 4m allowance only applies to a *tiled or slated pitched* roof. Confirm the quoted overall height, measured from finished ground level including the base.
- Condition 4 (external finishes must conform with the house) applies only to structures placed **to the side** of a house. The pod is going to the rear, so it doesn't bite — **cladding is a free choice**, cedar or composite or anything else. The long narrow side lane isn't a candidate location anyway.
- Condition 6: a personal gym is unambiguously incidental. A home office for your own remote employment is conventionally accepted as incidental too. What would *not* be incidental: clients or staff visiting, signage, or anything resembling a business premises.

The same 2026 instrument also raised the Class 1 house-extension exemption from 40m² to 45m², and separate instruments created a new **Class 3A** for habitable garden dwellings (32–45m², requires 14 days' notice to the council, expires 31 Dec 2030). Not your use case, but relevant if you ever want a granny flat — and note that a Class 3 structure eats into the combined allowance, so a 12m² pod now does constrain that option later.

### 2.2 Building Regulations — exempt for a gym; arguably not for an office

Governing law: Building Regulations 1997 (S.I. 497/1997), **Third Schedule (Exempted Buildings), Class 2**. Verbatim conditions:

1. Detached from any other building.
2. Floor area not exceeding **25 square metres**.
3. Height not exceeding 3m, or 4m with a pitched roof.
4. "used exclusively for recreational or storage purposes or the keeping of plants, birds or animals for domestic purposes and **shall not be used for the purposes of any trade or business** or for human habitation."

**A gym is recreational → fully exempt from the Building Regulations.** Clean.

**An office used for your employment is arguably "for the purposes of any trade or business"**, which would take it outside Class 2 and mean Parts A–M apply in full (structure, fire, ventilation, Part L energy conservation). In practice: enforcement of this against domestic garden offices is effectively nil, thousands exist, and nobody is coming to inspect. It matters for two reasons only — it means a supplier's claim to be "fully building-regs compliant" is usually unverified marketing, and it's a latent technical non-compliance you should know exists rather than discover later.

Since the gym is the primary plan, this is a non-issue for you. Worth knowing if the backup plan ever becomes the main plan.

**Note the new mismatch:** planning exemption is now 30m², but the Building Regulations exemption is still **25m²**. A pod between 25 and 30m² is planning-exempt but *not* Building-Regs-exempt. Several supplier blogs have not caught up with this. At 12m² you're inside both.

### 2.3 Commencement notice — not required

Governing law: Building Control Regulations 1997 (S.I. 496/1997), Article 7, as amended.

- Art. 7(1): Part II (commencement notices) applies only to buildings **"to which the Building Regulations apply."**
- Art. 7(2)(a): Part II does **not** apply where the works are exempted development for planning purposes **and** Part III (fire safety certificates) does not apply.
- Art. 7(2)(c): Part II is re-applied only to a new *dwelling*, or an extension to a dwelling **exceeding 45m²** (raised from 40m² on 27 July 2026 by S.I. 359/2026).

A detached 12m² garden gym is neither a dwelling nor an extension. **No commencement notice, no BCMS filing, no assigned certifier, no fee.**

Some pod-company blog posts state that a commencement notice is always required. That is wrong for your case — they have imported the rule that applies to the new Class 3A habitable units.

### 2.4 Electrical — the one place with real legal teeth

This is the part of the project with genuine statutory obligations, and the part most likely to be done badly.

- Under the Electricity Regulation Act 1999 (as amended), this is **"restricted electrical work"**. Since 1 October 2013 it must be carried out by a **Registered Electrical Contractor (REC)** on the Safe Electric scheme, regulated by the CRU. Unregistered work is a prosecutable offence.
- The REC **must issue you a Completion Certificate** for all but minor works. This is your proof the installation meets the National Rules for Electrical Installations (I.S. 10101). Get it in your hands before final payment.
- Correct method: a **dedicated circuit from the house consumer unit** — not a spur off an existing socket — in **steel wire armoured (SWA) cable**, buried **450–600mm** on a sand bed with yellow warning tape above, terminating in a **local consumer unit inside the pod** with RCD/RCBO protection.
- Your 2006 consumer unit probably has spare capacity and a spare way, but confirm it. Boards at capacity need upgrading first, which is a cost that surfaces on install day if nobody checked.

**Your existing shed supply:** unknown provenance, so treat it as suspect. It is quite likely a spur or unarmoured cable. Have an REC test it. Do not let anyone reuse it to save a trench unless it tests clean and gets certified — and have the REC safely disconnect it when the shed is removed.

**Ethernet:** do it, and do it now rather than later. It's priced at around €385 ex VAT as a line item (GardenRooms.ie publish this), and Berko include CAT6 in their standard package. Run it in the same trench in **separate ducting**, physically separated from the SWA. Retrofitting means re-digging the garden. Pull a spare draw-cord or empty duct at the same time while the trench is open — it costs almost nothing and buys you every future option.

### 2.5 Other obligations worth handling

- **Drainage and wayleaves.** You just bought, so your purchase pack should include a certified site layout showing the foul and surface-water runs. Check it before fixing the pod position and the cable trench route — you don't want to build over a foul run or inside a wayleave. A 2006 house will have separate foul and surface systems.
- **Roof runoff.** 12m² of new roof is a small volume but it needs a destination — a soakaway or a connection to the existing surface-water system. Don't let it discharge toward the boundary or the neighbour's side.
- **Home insurance.** Notify your insurer. Outbuildings are often covered only to a sub-limit, and gym equipment may need specifying. An uninsured €20k structure is an avoidable own goal.
- **VAT — ask explicitly.** Construction services are 13.5%, but Revenue's **two-thirds rule** says that if the VAT-exclusive cost of the goods exceeds two-thirds of the total VAT-exclusive price, the *whole* supply is taxed at 23%. Factory-built pods dropped in are more likely to hit 23%; on-site builds are more likely to stay at 13.5%. On a €20k job that's roughly €1,600 of difference. More importantly, **quotes in this sector are inconsistently quoted ex- and inc-VAT**, which is the single biggest cause of comparing quotes that aren't comparable.

## 4. Reputation and reviews — and why the data is thin

**The honest finding: review data in this sector is too sparse to rely on.** Most Irish operators have no Trustpilot presence at all. The Google review counts companies advertise on their own sites are self-reported and self-curated. I could not independently verify star ratings for most of them.

What I did find:

**Gardenhouse24 — Trustpilot 4.0 from 166 reviews** (85% 5-star, but **7% 1-star**). Positives: responsive office staff, tidy install crews, good value. Negative complaints are specific and concerning:
- A cabin that "didn't survive two winters", with roof damage in the January 2025 storms and deterioration of walls, windows and doors.
- Almost three months' wait for replacement doors, with repeated broken promises.
- A customer advised to remove house insulation, then told mid-project that installation services were no longer available, leaving unexpected costs and abandoned work.
- Repeated complaints of unresponsive aftercare.

**The Pod Factory — Trustpilot 3.5, but from only 2 reviews**, one of which is a detailed 1-star:
- 12-week promise became nearly 7 months.
- "Uncommunicative throughout. Had to follow up repeatedly, answers took 2 weeks+ on average."
- Delivered pod had missing fixtures, dents, chips, uneven tiling.
- Contractor "destroyed our garden", left debris, and caused a leaking pipe connection.
- Scheduled repairs never happened, with no explanation.

Two reviews is not a pattern, but that level of specificity is worth weighing.

**Better-signalled operators:** Modern Garden Rooms (trading since 2008, claims 5.0 from 100+ Google reviews), GardenRooms.ie (82 five-star Google reviews), Loghouse (4.6 Google), Garden Office Solutions (consistently positive, though with a 3–4 month queue).

### Commercial risk — this is the real exposure

You'll be asked for a substantial deposit on a company you can't easily assess. I found no garden-room-specific Irish insolvency, but the template for what this looks like is **DK Windows & Doors** (Dublin 12, trading since 2005, into liquidation December 2024): it had accumulated **€1.5m of customer deposits** and ran out of cash, having promoted a Black Friday deposit push days before closing. Customers became unsecured creditors — bottom of the queue. One couple faced losing close to €50,000.

Practical protections, in order of usefulness:
1. **Keep the deposit as small as the supplier will accept**, and stage the remaining payments against delivered milestones (base poured, unit delivered, electrics certified, snag list closed).
2. **Pay by credit card where possible** — chargeback is a far better remedy than being an unsecured creditor.
3. **Check CRO filings** (core.cro.ie) before paying: how long incorporated, are annual returns filed and on time, any prior insolvency among the directors. A ten-minute check.
4. **Never pay the final tranche before the Safe Electric Completion Certificate is in your hands** and the snag list is closed.
5. Be wary of an unusually large discount contingent on an unusually large up-front payment. That's the DK Windows pattern.

## 5. What differs between offerings — the questions that actually separate them

### Insulation is the single biggest real divide

This determines whether you have a year-round gym or an expensive shed you avoid in January.

- **40mm composite panel** (Adman, Steeltech base spec) is thin. It's fine for storage or occasional use; it is well below what a habitable wall requires, and it means higher heating costs and more condensation risk in a room where you'll be generating moisture.
- **150mm build-ups** (Modern Garden Rooms: 50mm PIR + 100mm Earthwool; Berko: 100mm wool + 50mm Kingspan) approach dwelling-wall performance.
- **SIPs** (Garden Office Solutions, Outer Space Pods) perform well for their thickness and are airtight, but airtightness makes ventilation more important, not less.

**Almost nobody publishes U-values.** Ask for them in writing, for walls, roof and floor separately. A supplier who can't produce them is telling you something.

### Scope boundaries — who does what

This is where quotes diverge most, and where "from €X" pricing misleads. Ask each supplier, in writing, which of these are in their number:

| Item | Typical cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete base / foundations | €90–150/m² → **~€1,500–2,500** for 12m²; ground screws €1,200–3,000 | Included by My Garden Room (turnkey) and GardenRooms.ie; **excluded** by Adman; unstated by most. Ground screws avoid a slab and are faster, but a slab is better under dropped weights. |
| Electrical cable from house | **~€900 ex VAT** (GardenRooms.ie published) to €1,500+ | Depends heavily on trench length and what it crosses. Paving or driveway crossings cost significantly more. |
| CAT6 Ethernet | **~€385 ex VAT** | Included as standard by Berko. |
| Internal fit-out (plaster, paint, flooring, sockets, lights, heating) | ~€5,000–9,000 if not included | This is most of the basic-vs-turnkey gap. |
| Delivery | ~€350 | Free within Leinster from several suppliers. |
| Skip / spoil / **old shed removal** | **~€350**, more if there's a slab to break out | **On the critical path** — the shed sits in the pod's footprint, so demolition and disconnection must complete before the base goes in. See §5.1. |
| External oiling (cedar) | €825–1,050 per application | **Recurring.** Cedar needs re-oiling. Fibre-cement and composite cladding don't — a real maintenance trade-off, not just aesthetics. |

### 5.1 The shed is on the critical path

The shed occupies the pod's footprint, so it isn't an optional tidy-up — demolition has to finish before the base goes in. That has a few practical consequences:

- **Keep it inside one contract.** Have the pod company own demolition, disposal, base and install as a single scope. If you split it, you create a gap where each party can blame the other for the ground not being ready, and you carry the schedule risk yourself.
- **Ask what's under the shed.** A 2006-era shed very likely sits on a slab or a bed of paving. Find out whether breaking it out and removing the spoil is in the quote, and who bears the cost if it turns out to be thicker than expected. Do not assume the old base is reusable — a shed base won't have the depth, reinforcement or level tolerance a pod needs, and any supplier willing to build on it without inspection is cutting a corner.
- **Trace the existing supply before anyone digs.** The shed has power of unknown provenance. Have the REC find and isolate it at the board before demolition, and identify its route. If it runs where the new trench will go, you want that known in advance rather than discovered with a spade. Its route may also tell you the cheapest path for the new SWA run.
- **Sequence the disconnection properly.** Shed supply isolated and made safe → shed demolished and removed → slab broken out and spoil taken away → ground checked and levelled → base laid. Ask for that sequence in the quote so the skip is on site once, not twice.

### Do they design for you?

Mostly no, and mostly you don't need it. For a 12m² exempt structure there's nothing to design around — no drawings to lodge, no certificates to file. Most suppliers do a free site visit, produce 3D visuals, and quote. Only the premium end (GardenRooms.ie, Concept Modular) does anything resembling design work. Don't pay for architectural input on this.

### Warranties

Wildly inconsistent, and most suppliers don't publish one at all. The outliers: Concept Modular's 50-year structural warranty, Garden Room Ireland's 25-year exterior guarantee. Get the warranty **in writing with the quote**, and check what it actually covers — structure only, or also the roof membrane, glazing units and electrics, which are what actually fail. Note the Gardenhouse24 complaint about a cabin failing within two winters: a warranty is only as good as the company still being there.

### Lead times

10–12 weeks (Adman, Berko) is typical. Garden Office Solutions has a 3–4 month queue before work starts. Steeltech claims install in under a day once manufactured. Given you have no urgency, use the lead time as leverage rather than a constraint — and treat a supplier promising something dramatically faster than the field with suspicion. The Pod Factory complaint was precisely a 12-week promise becoming 7 months.

## 6. Gym-specific — the few things that genuinely matter

Keeping this proportionate, as you asked. Four things, none of them exotic:

1. **Internal ceiling height.** The budget steel units have a **2.1m wall height** — that is genuinely tight for anything overhead, and it's the one dimension you cannot fix later. Ask for internal floor-to-ceiling height, not wall height or external height, and aim for ~2.4m.
2. **Floor build-up.** Dropped weights need a floor that doesn't flex or drum. A concrete slab with rubber matting is the simple, cheap answer. A timber deck on joists over ground screws is springier and noisier — fine for cardio and bodyweight, less good if you're deadlifting.
3. **Ventilation.** A well-insulated, airtight box with someone exercising in it gets hot and humid quickly, and the moisture has to go somewhere or it condenses. An openable window plus a small extract fan handles it. This is the most commonly skipped item and the most common regret.
4. **Heating.** With decent insulation, 12m² needs very little — a single panel heater is usually enough (Berko bundle a ~1.5kW smart heater). If the insulation is 40mm, you'll be paying for heat continuously instead. That's the insulation decision showing up as a running cost.

You don't need acoustic isolation, and I'd skip it. Normal construction with a decent floor mat is what almost everyone does.

## 7. Suggested shortlist and next step

Given your brief — normal spec, 12m², gym-primary, Dublin 5, no urgency — I'd get three quotes on an identical written specification from:

1. **Garden Office Solutions** — €19,950 for 12.5m², SIPs, cedar, triple glazing. Best-specified published price at your size.
2. **Modern Garden Rooms** — best published insulation build-up, Dublin/Leinster, trading since 2008. Quote-only, so this tests whether the spec comes at a sane price.
3. **Sprout Pod** — €18,500 for exactly 4m × 3m, turnkey, published price list, nationwide install. The pricing transparency is a genuine plus; the missing insulation spec is the thing to interrogate.

Worth a fourth quote from **Shanette** (Dublin 12 showroom, specify the **100mm** panel not the 40mm) as an Irish-manufactured steel alternative at likely lower cost, and from **Adman** at €11,250 purely as a price anchor — its 2.1m wall height makes it a poor gym, but knowing the floor of the market strengthens your position on the others.

**Ask every supplier the same written questions:**

- U-values for wall, roof and floor, separately.
- Internal floor-to-ceiling height.
- Overall external height from finished ground level (must be ≤3m unless tiled/slated pitched).
- Is the base included, and is it a slab or ground screws? What's the floor build-up?
- Is the supply cable from the house included? Who is the REC, and will I receive a Completion Certificate?
- Is CAT6 included or priced? Will you run separate ducting plus a spare draw-cord in the same trench?
- Is demolition and disposal of the existing 3m² shed included — **and breaking out whatever slab is under it, plus removing the spoil**? Who bears the cost if the slab is thicker than expected? Is safe disconnection of its existing supply by your REC included?
- Is the quote inclusive or exclusive of VAT, and at what rate — 13.5% or 23%?
- What is the written warranty, what does it cover, and for how long?
- Deposit amount, payment schedule, and what each tranche is tied to.
- Addresses of two or three installs in Dublin that are at least three years old.

That last one is the highest-value question on the list. In a sector where the review data is this thin, a pod that has survived three Irish winters is better evidence than any number of five-star ratings.
